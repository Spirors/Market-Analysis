Reading the map

Retail is the one group still adding fresh, leveraged exposure — record margin debt, growing at a pace with only three precedents this century.
Hedge funds are running a barbell: max conviction in the same five AI names, offset by rising shorts and hedges elsewhere. That's the most fragile posture on the board — long and leveraged, but only one bad print from a forced unwind, as July showed.
The options market is quietly buying insurance beneath a calm VIX — a classic "climbing a wall of worry" signature.
Insiders, Berkshire, and Burry (before closing his fund) all sit in the same corner: selling, waiting, or gone — the people with the least urgency to chase the trade aren't chasing it.

How rare is this leverage buildup?

Margin debt is the cleanest read on speculative fuel in the system — it's a direct claim on stock collateral, so it has to unwind through forced selling when prices fall. The current pace of growth has happened only three other times this century.

2000
Dot-com peak. Margin debt hit a then-record before the Nasdaq fell roughly 78% over the following two years.
2007
Housing/credit peak. A fresh margin-debt record preceded the 2007–09 crash, which erased more than half the S&P 500's value.
2021
Post-pandemic/meme-stock peak. Record leverage preceded the 2022 bear market.
Now — Jun 2026
$1.53T, +51.5% YoY — margin debt/GDP at 4.1% vs. a 50-year median of ~1.5%. The 4th time this century the buildup has moved this fast.

Second-level read: the consensus trade — long AI, buy every dip — is fully priced and universally known, which is exactly why it's not where the interesting information is. The interesting information is that almost nobody expressing that consensus is doing it with unhedged conviction except retail, on the fastest leverage buildup in three decades outside 2000, 2007 and 2021. Insiders, Berkshire, and hedge funds' own broader books are quietly de-risking or hedging beneath the same rally retail is levering into. That's not a call that the thesis is wrong — it's a read that the buying supporting current prices is thinner and more leveraged than the crowding into the trade would suggest, which is precisely the setup that turned an ordinary bad print (SK Hynix, July 2026) into a forced $20–45B liquidation.
